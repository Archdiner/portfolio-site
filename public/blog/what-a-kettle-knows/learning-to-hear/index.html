How Machines Learn to Hear

One kettle, five ways to train a model to listen, and why each one ends up hearing something different.

Before you read anything else, put on headphones if you have them and press play. Don't look for clues, just listen and make a guess. (Fair warning: it gets fairly loud around the ten-second mark.)

How did you know?

Someone flicks the kettle on about three seconds in. It rumbles, builds to a roar, boils for a minute and a half and clicks itself off at 1:39. Most people get it, or get close, within a few seconds, which is a little strange when you think about it. Nobody said the word "kettle" and there was no picture. All that reached you was air pushing on your eardrums for a couple of minutes.

You also picked up a lot more than a name. Without really trying, you probably noticed that it started with a click, that the noise grew for a while before settling into a steady roar, and that it stopped by itself with another click. If I asked you how long it boiled, you could give me a rough answer.

Play it again and watch those moments go by:

Those are two different skills: hearing what a sound is, and keeping track of what happened and for how long. People do both without noticing they're separate. A computer gets neither for free.

This is the second post in a series. The first, What a Kettle Knows, was about the gap between hearing a sound and keeping track of it over time. This one goes inside the hearing. How does a model learn to hear at all? And why do models trained in different ways end up hearing the same kettle differently? Every model here follows roughly the same path:

1.76 million numbers

A microphone measures how hard the air is pushing on it, over and over again. This recording took that measurement 16,000 times every second, and each measurement is just a single number, so the whole 1:50 clip is a list of about 1.76 million of them. Drag the slider to zoom in:

From far away you can see the shape of the event. Zoom into the boil and it turns to fuzz, and if you keep going you reach the individual numbers, each one a dot swinging up and down around zero. Nothing in this list says "kettle". There isn't even a number that means "loud". Loudness is how far the dots swing across hundreds of them.

A picture of sound

Almost every model in this post reads sound as a picture instead. Any sound can be treated as a mix of pitches playing at once, from low rumble to high hiss. A spectrogram splits the sound into those pitches and shows how strong each one is over time. Time runs left to right, pitch runs from low at the bottom to high at the top, and brighter means louder. Here's the kettle drawn both ways:

The switch shows up as a thin vertical line. A click is so short that it contains a bit of every pitch at once, so it lights up a whole column. The boil is a broad smear, because thousands of bursting bubbles make noise at every pitch all the time. Over the boil the top of the picture slowly dims as the roar gets duller.1

Cutting the picture into patches

Most modern audio models are transformers, the same kind of network behind chatbots like ChatGPT. A transformer reads a sequence of tokens, so the picture has to become one. The standard trick, borrowed from image models, is to cut the spectrogram into small squares called patches, flatten each one into a list of numbers, and treat each list as one token:

That last step, hiding most of the patches, is how most modern audio models teach themselves with no labels at all. You can't fill in a missing chunk of boil without learning what a boil sounds like. But "fill it in" can mean several different things, and that's where models start to differ.

Five ways to teach a model to hear

The listeners I spent the most time with can be sorted by two questions about how they were trained:

The trouble with comparing the real models directly is that they differ in everything at once: training data, size, architecture and training goal. So I set up a small lab where only the goal changes. There's one tiny transformer of about 20,000 parameters, and five kinds of kitchen sound cut into half-second windows: the kettle heating, the kettle boiling, pouring, stirring, and a spoon tapping a cup. It gets trained five times, and between runs exactly one line of the training loop changes.3

To make the result visible, every version has to squeeze each window down to just two numbers, so we can plot it directly. Each dot below is one window, colored by what it really is. The model never sees those colors, except in step 1. All of this is real training, run on a laptop processor.

Step 1: learn from labels

The most direct way is to tell the model what each window is and let it learn to guess the label. This is how the AudioSet taggers were trained, on two million YouTube clips labeled by people.

It works almost perfectly: 98% of new windows land closest to their own kind. But look at what it learned. It has five islands and no opinion about how they relate. Nothing tells it that heating and boiling are the same kettle, or that stirring and tapping are both a spoon on china. And it needed labels, which are slow and expensive to collect.

Step 2: redraw what's hidden

So drop the labels. Hide three quarters of each window's patches and ask the model to redraw them from the rest. This is the idea behind masked autoencoders, and behind Dasheng.

It sorts 80% of new windows correctly, without ever seeing a label. More interesting is the map itself. Pouring sits on its own, because nothing else sounds like it. Heating runs into the boil, which is right, because it's the same kettle getting louder. Stirring and the spoon tapping the cup overlap, which is also right, because both are a spoon hitting china.

The catch is that redrawing every pixel means spending effort on things that don't matter, like the exact grain of the hiss in a boil.

Step 3: name the code

BEATs' answer is to make the target coarser. First sort all the patches into a small codebook of typical shapes (16 here, a few thousand in BEATs). Then, instead of redrawing a hidden patch, the model only has to say which codebook entry it is, a bit like guessing a missing word instead of a missing photo.

This one sorts 71% of new windows. The heating and the boil merge into a single continuous path, and the spoon sounds spread out along the top. With only 16 codes, many different patches share a word, so the model can't tell them apart. In BEATs, the codebook is itself learned and improved over several rounds, which is how it gets past that.

Step 4: match a teacher

EAT goes a step further and drops fixed targets altogether. It keeps a second copy of the model, the teacher, which sees the whole window and updates slowly towards the student. The student sees only a quarter of the patches and has to predict what the teacher thinks about the rest. As the student improves, so does the teacher, and so do the targets.

This is the messiest map, sorting 69% of new windows, and it does something none of the others do. Every half-second of boil gets squeezed onto almost the same point, and so does every half-second of pouring, while the spoon sounds stay spread out. That's what this goal rewards: two views of the same steady sound should get the same answer. It's a tidy way to learn what doesn't change. It's also a risk, because a model can satisfy this goal by giving everything the same answer, which is called collapse. Real methods add safeguards against it (here, normalizing the teacher's answers), and the boil and pour are a mild version of it.

Step 5: copy an expert

The last way skips the guessing game and copies a model that already exists. The target is simply what a big trained model says about each window. Here the expert is the real Dasheng, run on the same windows. USAD learns this way from two experts at once, one for speech and one for general sound, and the small AudioSet taggers also learn partly by copying a bigger model.

Copying an expert sorts 91% of new windows, the best of the label-free runs, which is the point of it: a tiny model inherits much of what a model thousands of times its size learned. It also inherits that model's way of seeing. Heating flows into the boil, pouring sits apart and the spoon sounds stay close, because that's how Dasheng hears them.

So that's the lab: the same tiny network, the same sounds, and one changed line in each run, giving five different ears. Labels carve the world into the categories you gave them. Redrawing keeps detail, naming codes keeps the gist, matching a teacher learns what stays the same, and copying an expert inherits someone else's ear.

Do the real models agree?

A toy lab is only interesting if it says something about the real thing. Here are three real self-taught models, EAT, Dasheng and USAD, run on exactly the same five sounds. For comparison there's also the raw spectrogram, with no model at all:

Even the raw spectrogram sorts the windows somewhat, because pouring and boiling really do look different. Every real model does better, and Dasheng best of all, at 96%. Dasheng's map also looks a lot like the lab's redraw and copy-an-expert maps. EAT's biggest directions are the messiest, as its tiny counterpart's were.

The clearest real-world sign of the "match a teacher" habit is small differences going missing. Play these four taps:

To my ear, A and B are the same mug, C is a glass and D is metal. Here's how alike four listeners think each pair is, on a scale where 1 means identical:4

The recipe, which measures brightness and decay directly, hears the soft mug and the metal bowl as quite different. EAT puts all four taps almost on top of each other, just as the lab's teacher-matching model squeezed every half-second of boil onto one point. Four clips don't prove anything on their own, but it's the pattern the lab predicts.

Finally, here's how each way of training does on tasks that go well beyond five kitchen sounds:

No single way of training wins. The recipe is best at telling materials apart and does well at noticing a change, but knows nothing about voices. The label-trained tagger is cheap and good at kitchen events, the kind of thing it was labeled with, and weak at emotion in a voice, which no label described. Dasheng, trained to redraw, is best at that emotion. EAT is best at noticing when a scene changes, and worst at telling china from metal.

That's what I mean by models hearing differently. They share the same pipeline, and often the same kind of network. What sets them apart is mostly what they were asked to do while they learned.

What none of them can tell you

Every model in this post can tell the boil from the pour. Ask one how long the kettle boiled, though, and none of them has an answer. That gap between hearing and keeping time is what the first post was about.

  1. Your inner ear does something similar. The cochlea is a coiled tube that vibrates at different places for different pitches, so your brain receives sound already split up roughly the way a spectrogram is. The spectrograms here use 64 bands spaced the way hearing is, closer together at low pitches. ↩
  2. Real audio transformers typically use 16 × 16 patches on spectrograms with 128 pitch bands and 10 ms time steps, so each patch covers about a sixth of a second. ↩
  3. The lab model is a two-layer transformer with 32-number tokens, about 20,000 parameters, ending in two numbers per window. Each window is half a second of spectrogram (8 time steps × 64 bands) cut into 8 patches. Each run is 1,500 steps of 128 windows with Adam. "Hiding" masks 75% of the patches. The codebook has 16 entries from k-means over all patches. The teacher is a moving average of the student (0.99 per step), with its outputs normalized across each batch. The expert targets are the real Dasheng's outputs averaged over each window. Scores are on 180 windows held out from training, and count how often a window's two numbers sit closest to the average of its own kind. The script is build/lab.py. ↩
  4. These are the cosine similarities between clip embeddings, each averaged over its patches. Each tap was loudness-matched and trimmed to three quarters of a second first. ↩
  5. Materials is the similarity between the soft mug tap and the metal bowl, where lower means it hears more difference (only measured for four listeners). Kitchen events is the frame-level score on EPIC-SOUNDS (21 classes). Voice emotion is CREMA-D accuracy with the actors held out (chance 0.17). Noticing a change is the change-detection score on 125 clips (luck 0.19). Every number is in the appendix. ↩

Further reading

Here are the papers and datasets behind this post if you want to go deeper. It's not a survey of the field, just the things I actually used or leaned on.

  • Volhejn, 2025. Neural audio codecs: how to get audio into LLMs. The other half of this story: how to turn audio into tokens a language model can continue. This post is about how models learn to hear; that one is about how they learn to speak.
  • Dosovitskiy et al., 2020. An image is worth 16x16 words: transformers for image recognition at scale. Where cutting pictures into patches comes from.
  • Gong, Chung & Glass, 2021. AST: Audio Spectrogram Transformer. The same patch trick applied to spectrograms.
  • Huang et al., 2022. Masked autoencoders that listen. Hide most of the patches and redraw them: the idea behind Dasheng and lab step 2.
  • Baevski et al., 2022. Efficient self-supervised learning with contextualized target representations for vision, speech and language (data2vec 2.0). The "match the teacher" recipe that EAT builds on.
  • Koutini et al., 2021. Efficient training of audio transformers with patchout (PaSST). The big transformer the small AudioSet taggers learn to copy.
  • Hinton, Vinyals & Dean, 2015. Distilling the knowledge in a neural network. The original "copy an expert" paper.
  • Gemmeke et al., 2017. Audio Set: an ontology and human-labeled dataset for audio events. Two million YouTube clips with sound labels, which is what the small taggers learned from.
  • Schmid, Koutini & Widmer, 2023. Efficient large-scale audio tagging via transformer-to-CNN knowledge distillation. The compact taggers (mn04, mn10, mn20) in the charts.
  • Chen et al., 2022. BEATs: audio pre-training with acoustic tokenizers. The "name the code" model.
  • Chen et al., 2024. EAT: self-supervised pre-training with efficient audio transformer. The model that hears every tap as nearly the same, and the one the Ear is fine-tuned from.
  • Dinkel et al., 2024. Scaling up masked audio encoder learning for general audio classification. Dasheng. (Its 0.74 emotion score in these posts is from my own test on acted voices, not from this paper.)
  • Huh et al., 2023. EPIC-SOUNDS: a large-scale dataset of actions that sound. The kitchen events column of the report card.
  • Cao et al., 2014. CREMA-D: crowd-sourced emotional multimodal actors dataset. The emotion-in-a-voice column of the report card.
