What a Kettle Knows

How different models learn to hear the same kettle, and why each one ends up noticing different things.

Before you read anything else, put on headphones if you have them and press play. Don't look for clues, just listen and make a guess. (Fair warning: it gets fairly loud around the ten-second mark.)

How did you know?

Someone flicks the kettle on about three seconds in. It rumbles, builds to a roar, boils for a minute and a half and clicks itself off at 1:39. Most people get it, or get close, within a few seconds, which is a little strange when you think about it. Nobody said the word "kettle" and there was no picture. All that reached you was air pushing on your eardrums for a couple of minutes.

You also picked up a lot more than a name. Without really trying, you probably noticed that it started with a click, that the noise grew for a while before settling into a steady roar, and that it stopped by itself with another click. If I asked you how long it boiled, you could give me a rough answer, and if I asked whether anyone poured the water afterwards you'd say no.

Play it again and watch those moments go by:

Those are two different skills: putting a name on a sound, and keeping track of what happened and for how long. People do both without noticing they're separate. A computer gets neither for free.

This post is my first real dive into audio research. What I found most interesting wasn't any single score. It was that every model I tried heard this kettle differently, and that the differences trace back to how each one was trained. So this is a tour of how machines hear: how sound becomes something a model can read, six different ways of teaching a model to listen, and what each of those ends up noticing and missing. Every model here follows roughly the same path:

Why care about a kettle? Because the questions people actually ask about a home are mostly about time. Did I leave the stove on? How long has the washing machine been beeping? A small device that listens could answer those without putting a camera in anyone's kitchen, but only if its way of hearing keeps track of time. Keep that in mind, because it's the test most of these models will fail.

1.76 million numbers

A microphone measures how hard the air is pushing on it, over and over again. This recording took that measurement 16,000 times every second, and each measurement is just a single number, so the whole 1:50 clip is a list of about 1.76 million of them. Drag the slider to zoom in:

From far away you can see the shape of the event. Zoom into the boil and it turns to fuzz, and if you keep going you reach the individual numbers, each one a dot swinging up and down around zero.

Nothing in this list says "kettle". There isn't even a number that means "loud": loudness is how far the dots swing across hundreds of them. The list keeps absolutely everything, and almost none of it matters.

How much can we throw away?

A good first experiment is to see how much we can lose before the kettle stops sounding like a kettle. Here are the same twenty seconds three ways: the original, a version with half as many numbers per second (about what a phone call carries), and a version with every pitch above 4,000 Hz stripped out, which is roughly the top note on a piano.

It gets duller and the hiss of the boil goes first, but with most of the detail gone it's still obviously a kettle. The picture under the player shows what changed, and it's the form almost every model in this post actually reads.

A picture of sound

Any sound can be treated as a mix of pitches playing at once, from low rumble to high hiss. A spectrogram splits the sound into those pitches and shows how strong each one is over time. Time runs left to right, like in the waveform, pitch runs from low at the bottom to high at the top, and brighter means louder. Here's the kettle again, drawn both ways:

The switch shows up as a thin vertical line. A click is so short that it contains a bit of every pitch at once, so it lights up a whole column. The boil is a broad smear, because thousands of bursting bubbles make noise at every pitch all the time. Over the boil the top of the picture slowly dims as the roar gets duller, and even after the click-off it doesn't go dark straight away, because the water keeps settling for a few seconds.

When we cut the quality in the last section, we were really shaving the top off this picture. The kettle survived because most of what makes it sound like a kettle lives in the bottom half.1

A picture also has shapes, and simple geometry gets you surprisingly far. Here's a different recording: hot water going into a cup, with a spoon knocking against the side. Steady sounds like running water draw horizontal stripes, because the same pitches carry on over time. Sharp sounds like a spoon hitting china draw vertical lines, because every pitch flares up for an instant. A classic trick splits the picture into its horizontal and vertical parts,2 with no learning involved at all:

It isn't perfect (some water leaks into the clicks), but the spoon pops right out. Hold on to the idea that a picture of sound has a time axis you can read clicks off, because the models are going to struggle with exactly that.

Cutting the picture into patches

Most of the big models in this post are transformers, the same kind of network behind chatbots like ChatGPT. A transformer reads a sequence of tokens, so the picture has to become one. The standard trick, borrowed from image models, is to cut the spectrogram into small squares called patches, flatten each one into a list of numbers, and treat each list as one token.3

That's also where the training differences start. Click through the steps:

Hiding most of the patches and asking the model to fill them back in is how most modern audio models teach themselves, with no labels at all. It works because you can't fill in a missing chunk of boil without learning what a boil sounds like. But "fill it in" can mean very different things. Dasheng has to redraw the hidden patch. BEATs only has to name it, choosing from a few thousand code words. EAT has to match what a slowly updated copy of itself thinks about the patch, and about the whole clip. Those small differences in the goal turn out to produce noticeably different ears.

Watching training happen

The claim that the training goal shapes what a model hears is easy to say, so I wanted to see it. I took a tiny network and trained it twice on half-second windows from this post's recordings: the kettle heating, the kettle boiling, pouring, stirring, and a spoon tapping a cup. Once with labels, where it's told which sound each window is. Once with no labels, by hiding 75% of each window's patches and asking it to redraw them, a miniature Dasheng. Press play:

With labels, the five sounds pull cleanly apart, because that's exactly what the network was asked to do. It ends up with five tidy islands and no opinion about how the islands relate.

Without labels, the network arranges the sounds by how they sound. Pouring ends up off on its own, because nothing else here sounds like it. Stirring and the spoon tapping a cup end up overlapping, which is fair, since both are a spoon hitting china. And the crackly heating phase lands partly with the boil and partly with the clinks. Nobody told it any of that. It found the structure that helped it redraw the missing patches.

Does the same thing happen in real models? I ran three of the big self-taught ones, EAT, Dasheng and USAD, on exactly the same five kinds of sound. (BEATs wasn't available in a form I could run here.) For comparison, there's also the raw spectrogram with no model at all:

Even the raw spectrogram sorts the windows somewhat (76%), because pouring and boiling really do look different. Each learned model does better, with EAT at 90%, USAD at 91% and Dasheng at 96%, and none of them was ever told what these sounds are. Their pictures also differ, though: each one decided different directions were the important ones. That's the training goal showing through.

The big models do the same thing at a vastly larger scale, and you can see the effect in what they think sounds alike. Here's a small test, so play the four taps first:

To my ear, A and B are clearly the same mug, C is a glass and D is metal. To compare clips, each model turns a clip into one list of numbers, an embedding, by averaging over its patches, and then we check how close two lists are. Here's how close four of the listeners put each pair, on a scale where 1 means identical:

All four agree that the two mug taps are the closest pair, but they spread the rest very differently. The recipe, which measures brightness and decay directly, hears the soft mug and the metal bowl as quite different. EAT puts all four taps almost on top of each other. One plausible reason is its training goal: matching a teacher's view of the whole clip rewards ignoring small differences, and the ring of china against metal is a small difference. Four clips don't prove that, but it's the same pattern the tiny model showed: what you ask a model to do decides which differences it keeps.

It also shows up at scale. Rank the same models on two tasks, finding events in kitchen recordings and hearing emotion in acted speech, and the order flips:

The small AudioSet taggers keep up with BEATs on kitchen sounds, because those sounds are close to the labels they were trained on, and then they drop to the bottom on emotion, which no label described. Dasheng and USAD, whose training rewarded keeping acoustic detail or copying a speech teacher, go the other way. There's no single "best ear". There's only best at what.

Teaching it fifteen words

None of these models outputs a name out of the box. To get names, you fine-tune: add a small layer on top that maps the model's view of each moment onto a list of labels, then keep training on labelled examples. I did this with EAT, on RealDESED, a set of real home recordings labelled with fifteen everyday sounds: footsteps, dishes, doors, a light switch, a vacuum, a coffee machine and the rest. I tried three learning rates, ten passes over the data (epochs) each, on one rented A100 GPU. Each epoch took about two minutes, so the whole thing ran in under an hour. Here's the run:

The highest learning rate learns fastest and ends best. All three runs show the classic shape: loss drops steeply, flattens out, and then ticks back up on the last epoch, a hint that more training wouldn't have helped. I'll call the result the Ear. Here's everything it reported while listening to our kettle:

A light switch, then running water, then a coffee machine, and then, for most of the boil, a vacuum cleaner.

It's easy to laugh, but look at the timing. "Light switch" lands at 2.7 seconds, exactly when the kettle clicks on. "Running water" and "coffee machine" cover the heating, when a kettle really does sound like water being churned around. "Kettle" isn't one of the Ear's fifteen words, so it reached for the nearest ones it had. That's fine-tuning's trade: you get names, but only the ones you taught.

The more interesting part is what it didn't say. After about 53 seconds it goes quiet, even though the kettle keeps boiling for another 45. And nothing in its output connects the click at the start to the quiet at the end, or says that one object was on the whole time.

Finding repeats

Before going further, I wanted to know which basic timing skills the models already had. I put together 345 short clips where something changes partway through (a sound starts, stops, or gives way to another) and asked two things. Did the model notice the change? And could it find how many times a sound repeated?

The repeat test works like this. Take a two-second example of the sound, average the model's view of it into a single vector, then slide that vector along the recording, frame by frame, and count the peaks where the two match. You can try the same idea on the raw waveform of a bouncing ball:

On the raw waveform, matching works almost perfectly. Each bounce lines up with the first one and makes a sharp peak. Here are the scores across all 345 clips:

Noticing a change is fine: EAT scores 0.78 and the recipe 0.74. Finding repeats is a wipeout. Waveform matching gets 0.94, and no model gets above 0.14, which is barely better than luck.

The recording still had its time steps, so time wasn't thrown away.4 Bigger didn't help either: the 90-million-parameter models did no better than the one with a million. So why did they fail?

With the real models running, I could finally look. I made a small re-creation of the test: a mug tapped six times over twenty seconds of our kettle boiling. A two-second example around the first tap gets averaged into a single vector, as in the test, and then slid along each model's frames:

Look at where the taps are. In every model's curve they're dips, not peaks. The two-second example is mostly boil with a fraction of a second of tap in it, so its average looks like boil, and the moments that match it least are the taps. Counting peaks, every model finds nothing. Switch to counting dips, and USAD finds all six taps and nothing else, and Dasheng finds all six plus two false alarms. EAT finds three dips and none of them is a tap. They sit at the start of each of its ten-second windows, for a reason the next figure shows.

In this re-creation, at least, the models heard the taps perfectly well, and the peak-counting was looking the wrong way. I haven't rerun the full 345-clip test with dips yet, so I can't say this explains all of the failure, but it's the first thing I'd check.

The waveform shows the opposite problem. It nails the clean bouncing ball, but with a kettle boiling underneath, the later taps barely match the first one, because the noise on top of each is different. It finds seven peaks, and only two of them are taps, one being the tap it was copied from.

EAT's poor showing has a second cause, which you can see if you compare every moment of the kettle with every other moment:

The spectrogram's map follows the sound itself: after the click, one big block while the boil is loudest, then a second block once the roar dulls and the kettle switches off. EAT and Dasheng show a grid instead. I fed them the recording ten seconds at a time, and their frames turn out to remember where they sit inside each ten-second window. During the steady boil, two EAT frames from the same spot in different windows are 0.98 alike, against 0.89 for frames half a window apart. For Dasheng it's 0.77 against 0.54.

That's a side effect of how transformers work. Each patch is told its position so the model can keep track of order, and some of that position survives all the way to the output. For classifying a clip it barely matters, but for matching a sample against a long recording it's noise. EAT also produces only about six frames a second, which is coarse for a tap. USAD, fed thirty seconds at a time, shows a much weaker version (0.91 against 0.88). Its big blocks are the thirty-second windows themselves.

What about a bigger model?

At this point you might reasonably ask why I'm fiddling with listeners like these at all. There are now large audio-language models that you can hand a recording and question in plain English. They're built like this:

So I tried one, Audio Flamingo 3, on a much harder version of the kettle question: a week of audio recorded continuously in one person's home,6 with 298 questions like whether the vacuum was on at a given moment, since when, for how long, and how many times. Each question came with ten minutes of audio.

It mostly answered "none". Overall it scored 0.19, below the 0.26 you get from a baseline that ignores the audio and always gives the most common answer. Some of that was my prompt and my scoring, so I wouldn't read much into the exact number. But it shows something about the design: the language model only sees what the encoder hands it, and nothing in either half was trained to keep time across ten minutes.

Knowing when to listen

Training data leaves fingerprints in other ways, too. Whisper, a popular speech-to-text model, learned from hundreds of thousands of hours of internet audio paired with whatever text came with it, much of it subtitles. I ran the small version over an hour of that same home with nobody in it, and in 49 of those 60 minutes it produced text. It kept writing the same sentence:

Thank you very much for watching this video and I will see you in much for watching.

A common explanation is that the end of an online video is often quiet, and the subtitles over that quiet often say exactly this, so to Whisper an empty room sounds like the end of a video. Putting a small voice-activity detector in front of it, so that Whisper only hears the parts where someone is actually talking, made the invented words disappear. With speech mixed into the home's background noise, the word error rate fell from 25% to 4%. Whisper itself didn't change at all.

Keeping a diary

Which brings us back to the questions about time. The approach I ended up with doesn't ask a model to remember audio at all. A listener writes a diary instead: every time something changes, it notes what turned on or off, and when, and it smooths over the flicker so that one long boil becomes one entry rather than thirty.7

This isn't a new idea, and I didn't come up with it. In video, Socratic Models (2022) turned first-person footage into a running text log and answered questions by reading it, and a 2026 paper does the same with long audio recordings. (Links are in the further reading.) Once the diary exists, questions about time become arithmetic on a list. Ask the kettle's diary something:

Back in the week-long home recording, a listener built on BEATs, frozen, with a small layer on top for household activities, wrote the diary itself. The same 298 questions were then answered from that diary:

The diary gets 0.87 overall, and it's weakest on "how many times?", which is the repeat problem again. The surprising part was which half did the work. Handing the same diary to a general language model (Qwen) and asking in plain English only moved its score from 0.25 to 0.34. What got 0.87 was answering with small exact queries that look things up and subtract. Writing things down turned out to be only half of it. The other half was doing the arithmetic properly.

Treat this as a demonstration rather than a benchmark. It's one home, and the diary's vocabulary was set up for that home's activities, which Audio Flamingo didn't get. The whole system runs on an ordinary laptop processor. Plenty didn't work along the way, including a test where I'd swapped "added" and "removed", and a result that turned out to be a home memorising itself. The full list is in the appendix.

Back to the kettle

Let's play it one last time, and this time watch the diary:

So what does a kettle know? It depends who's listening, and how they were taught. To the list of numbers it's 1.76 million values. The recipe measures how bright and loud it is. A model taught to fill in hidden patches knows what a boil sounds like, but not when this one started. The Ear, taught fifteen words, calls it a vacuum cleaner for fifty seconds. And the diary knows it switched on at 0:02.7, boiled for 86 seconds and switched itself off, which is all anyone was going to ask it.

Every one of those is a choice someone made about what a model should care about, including the diary, whose format I chose myself. Rich Sutton's bitter lesson says hand-made choices like that tend to lose, eventually, to models that learn for themselves what's worth keeping. The tiny model above is a small version of that: nobody told it that stirring and tapping are both a spoon on china, and it worked that out from the sound.

That's where I'm heading next. I want to understand self-supervised training from the inside, why redrawing, naming and matching a teacher produce such different ears, and whether some version of it could learn to keep time. And then I want to go the other way, to diffusion models that generate sound instead of describing it.

Notes

  1. Your inner ear does something similar. The cochlea is a coiled tube that vibrates at different places for different pitches, so your brain receives sound already split up roughly the way a spectrogram is. The spectrograms here use 64 bands spaced the way hearing is, closer together at low pitches. ↩
  2. It's called harmonic–percussive source separation (HPSS). Learned versions exist, but HPSS is cheap, needs no training data, and is easy to hear. ↩
  3. Real audio transformers typically use 16 × 16 patches on spectrograms with 128 pitch bands and 10 ms time steps, so each patch covers about a sixth of a second. The patches in the figure are much bigger so they're visible. ↩
  4. Inside BEATs and EAT, the patches at each moment are averaged across pitch, but the sequence over time is kept. The two-second example is the only part that gets averaged into a single vector. Peaks had to be at least 1.5 seconds apart, and the threshold was chosen on the first 50 items and scored on the last 50. ↩
  5. The re-creation uses 22 seconds of the kettle's boil with a hard mug tap added six times, about twice as loud as the boil's peaks. The example is the two seconds from 1.0 to 3.0, around the first tap. "Stands well clear" means more than 2.5 robust standard deviations from the curve's median. The models are EAT, Dasheng and USAD2-Small run on CPU, and the script is in the post's folder. ↩
  6. SINS, a public dataset recorded continuously in one home for a week, with daily activities like cooking, washing up and vacuuming labelled. The questions cover days four to seven. ↩
  7. The smoothing is a Viterbi pass. Given second-by-second guesses about whether something is on, it finds the most likely sequence of on and off stretches, so a single noisy second can't split one boil into two. ↩

Further reading

Here are the papers and datasets behind this post if you want to go deeper. It's not a survey of the field, just the things I actually used or leaned on.

  • Volhejn, 2025. Neural audio codecs: how to get audio into LLMs. The other half of this story: how to turn audio into tokens a language model can continue. If this post is about remembering, that one is about speaking.
  • Dosovitskiy et al., 2020. An image is worth 16x16 words: transformers for image recognition at scale. Where cutting pictures into patches comes from.
  • Gong, Chung & Glass, 2021. AST: Audio Spectrogram Transformer. The same patch trick applied to spectrograms.
  • Huang et al., 2022. Masked autoencoders that listen. Hide most of the patches and redraw them: the idea behind Dasheng and the tiny model.
  • Baevski et al., 2022. Efficient self-supervised learning with contextualized target representations for vision, speech and language (data2vec 2.0). The "match the teacher" recipe that EAT builds on.
  • Koutini et al., 2021. Efficient training of audio transformers with patchout (PaSST). The big transformer the small AudioSet taggers learn to copy.
  • Gemmeke et al., 2017. Audio Set: an ontology and human-labeled dataset for audio events. Two million YouTube clips with sound labels, which is what the small taggers learned from.
  • Schmid, Koutini & Widmer, 2023. Efficient large-scale audio tagging via transformer-to-CNN knowledge distillation. The compact taggers (mn04, mn10, mn20) in the charts.
  • Chen et al., 2022. BEATs: audio pre-training with acoustic tokenizers. One of the large self-taught listeners, and the one the diary system builds on.
  • Chen et al., 2024. EAT: self-supervised pre-training with efficient audio transformer. The model that hears every tap as nearly the same, and the one the Ear is fine-tuned from.
  • Dinkel et al., 2024. Scaling up masked audio encoder learning for general audio classification. Dasheng, the best of the bunch at hearing emotion in a voice.
  • Goel et al., 2025. Audio Flamingo 3: advancing audio intelligence with fully open large audio language models. The big model we asked about the week.
  • Dekkers et al., 2017. The SINS database for detection of daily activities in a home environment using an acoustic sensor network. The week in one home.
  • Radford et al., 2022. Robust speech recognition via large-scale weak supervision. Whisper.
  • Fitzgerald, 2010. Harmonic/percussive separation using median filtering. The smooth-and-sharp trick.
  • Rabiner, 1989. A tutorial on hidden Markov models and selected applications in speech recognition. Still the friendliest introduction to the Viterbi smoothing behind the diary.
  • Huh et al., 2023. EPIC-SOUNDS: a large-scale dataset of actions that sound. The kitchen recordings behind the short-events results.
  • Cao et al., 2014. CREMA-D: crowd-sourced emotional multimodal actors dataset. The acted voices behind the emotion scores in the appendix.

On writing things down instead of remembering:

  • Zeng et al., 2022. Socratic Models: composing zero-shot multimodal reasoning with language. Turns first-person video into a text log and answers questions by reading it. The earliest clear version of the diary idea I know of.
  • 2024. User-in-the-loop evaluation of multimodal LLMs for activity assistance. Finds that the text-log approach beats models looking at frames directly when a task needs a long history, including in a live cooking study.
  • 2024. VideoAgent: a memory-augmented multimodal agent for video understanding (ECCV). A structured memory of events and objects that a language model queries with tools.
  • 2026. Event-grounded question answering over long audio via structured retrieval. The audio version: long recordings become timestamped events in a database, with smart-home monitoring as a target use. The closest thing to this post's diary.
  • 2026. TAG-Bench: benchmarking temporal audio grounding in large audio language models. Twenty-one systems asked when sounds happen, with the best model counting repeated sounds correctly under 14% of the time. Independent evidence for the counting problem.
  • Sutton, 2019. The bitter lesson. Why a diary format designed by a person is probably a stepping stone, not the destination.

Method
