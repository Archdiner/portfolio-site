What a Kettle Knows

One recording of a kettle, heard five different ways by a computer, and what each way of listening keeps and throws away.

Before you read anything else, put on headphones if you have them and press play. Don't look for clues, just listen and make a guess. (Fair warning: it gets fairly loud around the ten-second mark.)

How did you know?

Someone flicks the kettle on about three seconds in. It rumbles, builds to a roar, boils for a minute and a half and clicks itself off at 1:39. Most people get it, or get close, within a few seconds, which is a little strange when you think about it. Nobody said the word "kettle" and there was no picture. All that reached you was air pushing on your eardrums for a couple of minutes.

You also picked up a lot more than a name. Without really trying, you probably noticed that it started with a click, that the noise grew for a while before settling into a steady roar, and that it stopped by itself with another click. If I asked you how long it boiled, you could give me a rough answer, and if I asked whether anyone poured the water afterwards you'd say no.

Play it again and watch those moments go by:

Those are two different skills: putting a name on a sound, and keeping track of what happened and for how long. People do both without noticing they're separate. A computer gets neither for free. Someone has to decide how it should look at sound, and every way of looking makes some things easy to see and others invisible.

This post is my first real dive into audio research, and it's less a single result than a tour. We'll take this one kettle and look at it the way different models do: as a list of numbers, as a picture, as a point in space, as a list of labels and finally as a diary. At each stop I'll try to show what that way of listening keeps, what it throws away, and what happened when I tried it.

Why care about a kettle? Because the questions people actually ask about a home are mostly about time. Did I leave the stove on? How long has the washing machine been beeping? A small device that listens could answer those without putting a camera in anyone's kitchen, but only if its way of hearing keeps track of time. As we'll see, most of them don't.

1. A list of numbers

A microphone measures how hard the air is pushing on it, over and over again. This recording took that measurement 16,000 times every second, and each measurement is just a single number, so the whole 1:50 clip is a list of about 1.76 million of them. Drag the slider to zoom in:

From far away you can see the shape of the event. Zoom into the boil and it turns to fuzz, and if you keep going you reach the individual numbers, each one a dot swinging up and down around zero.

Nothing in this list says "kettle". There isn't even a number that means "loud": loudness is how far the dots swing across hundreds of them. The list keeps absolutely everything, and that's the problem, because almost none of it matters. Every way of listening after this one is a decision about what to throw away.

How much can we throw away?

A good first experiment is to see how much we can lose before the kettle stops sounding like a kettle. Here are the same twenty seconds three ways: the original, a version with half as many numbers per second (about what a phone call carries), and a version with every pitch above 4,000 Hz stripped out, which is roughly the top note on a piano.

It gets duller and the hiss of the boil goes first, but with most of the detail gone it's still obviously a kettle. The picture under the player shows what changed, and it's our second way of listening.

2. A picture

Any sound can be treated as a mix of pitches playing at once, from low rumble to high hiss. A spectrogram splits the sound into those pitches and shows how strong each one is over time. Time runs left to right, like in the waveform, pitch runs from low at the bottom to high at the top, and brighter means louder. Here's the kettle again, drawn both ways:

The switch shows up as a thin vertical line. A click is so short that it contains a bit of every pitch at once, so it lights up a whole column. The boil is a broad smear, because thousands of bursting bubbles make noise at every pitch all the time. Over the boil the top of the picture slowly dims as the roar gets duller, and even after the click-off it doesn't go dark straight away, because the water keeps settling for a few seconds.

When we cut the quality in the last section, we were really shaving the top off this picture. The kettle survived because most of what makes it sound like a kettle lives in the bottom half.1

A picture also has shapes, and simple geometry gets you surprisingly far. Here's a different recording: hot water going into a cup, with a spoon knocking against the side. Steady sounds like running water draw horizontal stripes, because the same pitches carry on over time. Sharp sounds like a spoon hitting china draw vertical lines, because every pitch flares up for an instant. A classic trick splits the picture into its horizontal and vertical parts,2 with no learning involved at all:

It isn't perfect (some water leaks into the clicks), but the spoon pops right out. The picture keeps when things happen and what kind of sound they are. What it doesn't do is tell you that two pictures are of the same thing.

3. A point in space

The same mug tapped softly and tapped hard makes two quite different pictures, and we want something that shrugs and says "same mug". The usual answer is to train a model that turns any clip into a short list of numbers, called an embedding. You can think of it as a point in space, arranged so that clips which sound alike land close together. Here's a small test, so play the four taps first:

To my ear, A and B are clearly the same mug, C is a glass and D is metal. Here's how close four different listeners put each pair, on a scale where 1 means identical:

The "recipe" has never been trained on anything. It measures a few dozen things a sound engineer would care about, like brightness and how quickly a sound dies away. The other three are large models that taught themselves by listening to millions of clips.3

All four agree that the two mug taps are the closest pair, but they spread the rest very differently. The recipe hears the soft mug and the metal bowl as quite different. EAT puts all four taps almost on top of each other. Four clips don't prove much, but they show what an embedding really is: a decision about which differences to ignore.

They also show what a single point can't hold. Try counting the bounces of this ball:

Easy for you, and impossible for a single point, because the point describes the whole clip at once, a bit like summing up a song in one sentence. You can say it's upbeat and has a guitar in it, but you've lost how many times the chorus came round. When I tested summary embeddings from eight different models on a few hundred clips like this, none of them could count. A decades-old trick that slides the waveform along a copy of itself and notes where it lines up4 almost always could.

4. A list of labels

Embeddings say what something sounds like. To get names, you train a model to output labels. I took EAT, the same model that thought all four taps sounded alike, and fine-tuned it to label fifteen everyday home sounds, a few seconds at a time: footsteps, dishes, doors, a light switch, a vacuum, a coffee machine and the rest. I'll call it the Ear. Here's everything it reported while listening to our kettle:

A light switch, then running water, then a coffee machine, and then, for most of the boil, a vacuum cleaner.

It's easy to laugh, but look at the timing. "Light switch" lands at 2.7 seconds, exactly when the kettle clicks on. "Running water" and "coffee machine" cover the heating, when a kettle really does sound like water being churned around. "Kettle" isn't one of the Ear's fifteen words, so it reached for the nearest ones it had.

The more interesting part is what it didn't say. After about 53 seconds it goes quiet, even though the kettle keeps boiling for another 45. And nothing in its output connects the click at the start to the quiet at the end, or says that one object was on the whole time. Labels give you names and rough times, a few seconds at a time, but they don't give you the story.

What about a bigger model?

At this point you might reasonably ask why I'm fiddling with listeners like the Ear at all. There are now large audio-language models that you can hand a recording and question in plain English. So I tried one, Audio Flamingo 3, on a much harder version of the kettle question: a week of audio recorded in one person's home,5 with questions like whether the vacuum was on at a given moment, since when, and for how long.

It mostly answered "none". Overall it did worse than a baseline that ignores the audio and always gives the most common answer. Some of that was my prompt and my scoring, so I wouldn't read much into the exact number. But it was a good reminder that a model which can name a sound in a short clip isn't necessarily keeping track of anything over ten minutes, let alone a week.

5. A diary

The last way of listening is the one I'd been building towards: instead of asking a model to remember audio, have the listener keep a diary. Every time something changes, it writes down what turned on or off, and when. It also smooths over the flicker, so that one long boil becomes one entry rather than thirty.6

This isn't a new idea, and I didn't come up with it. In video, Socratic Models (2022) turned first-person footage into a running text log and answered questions by reading it, and a 2026 paper does the same with long audio recordings. (Links are in the further reading.) Once the diary exists, questions about time become arithmetic on a list. Ask the kettle's diary something:

Back in the week-long home recording, a listener built on BEATs (another of the self-taught models) wrote the diary itself, and it answered most of the questions correctly. It was weakest, unsurprisingly, on "how many times?". The whole thing runs on an ordinary laptop processor.

The surprising part was which half did the work. Handing the same diary to a general language model and asking in plain English only helped a little. What worked was answering from the diary with small exact queries that look things up and subtract. Writing things down turned out to be only half of it. The other half was doing the arithmetic properly.

That's one home, with a vocabulary chosen for its activities, so treat it as a demonstration rather than a benchmark. Plenty didn't work along the way either, including a test where I'd swapped "added" and "removed" and a result that turned out to be a home memorising itself. The full list is in the appendix.

Knowing when to listen

One more way of listening is to decide when not to. Whisper, a popular speech-to-text model, is known for making words up when nobody is talking. I ran it over an hour of that same home with nobody in it, and it kept writing the same sentence:

Thank you very much for watching this video and I will see you in much for watching.

Putting a small voice-activity detector in front of it, so that Whisper only hears the parts where someone is actually talking, made the invented words disappear. Whisper itself didn't change at all.

Back to the kettle

Let's play it one last time, and this time watch the diary:

So what does a kettle know? It depends who's listening. To the list of numbers it's 1.76 million values and nothing else. The picture knows where the clicks are. The point in space knows roughly what it sounds like, but not when anything happened. The Ear knows it's a vacuum cleaner, for about fifty seconds. And the diary knows it switched on at 0:02.7, boiled for 86 seconds and switched itself off, which is all anyone was going to ask it.

Every one of those is a choice someone made about what to keep, including the diary, whose format I chose myself. Rich Sutton's bitter lesson says hand-made choices like that tend to lose, eventually, to models that learn for themselves what's worth keeping. That's where I'm heading next. BEATs and EAT never saw a label: they learned by hiding pieces of audio and predicting what was missing, which is called self-supervised training. I want to understand how that works from the inside, and then go the other way, to diffusion models that generate sound instead of describing it.

Notes

  1. Your inner ear does something similar. The cochlea is a coiled tube that vibrates at different places for different pitches, so your brain receives sound already split up roughly the way a spectrogram is. The spectrograms here use 64 bands spaced the way hearing is, closer together at low pitches. ↩
  2. It's called harmonic–percussive source separation (HPSS). Learned versions exist, but HPSS is cheap, needs no training data, and is easy to hear. ↩
  3. BEATs, EAT and USAD are self-supervised models: they learned by hiding parts of recordings and predicting what was missing, without anyone labelling the sounds. The similarity is the cosine between their clip embeddings. Each tap was loudness-matched and trimmed to three quarters of a second first. ↩
  4. Normalised cross-correlation. If a sound repeats, the waveform lines up with a shifted copy of itself once per repeat. ↩
  5. SINS, a public dataset recorded continuously in one home for a week, with daily activities like cooking, washing up and vacuuming labelled. ↩
  6. The smoothing is a Viterbi pass. Given second-by-second guesses about whether something is on, it finds the most likely sequence of on and off stretches, so a single noisy second can't split one boil into two. ↩

Further reading

Here are the papers and datasets behind this post if you want to go deeper. It's not a survey of the field, just the things I actually used or leaned on.

  • Volhejn, 2025. Neural audio codecs: how to get audio into LLMs. The other half of this story: how to turn audio into tokens a language model can continue. If this post is about remembering, that one is about speaking.
  • Gemmeke et al., 2017. Audio Set: an ontology and human-labeled dataset for audio events. Two million YouTube clips with sound labels, which is what the small taggers learned from.
  • Schmid, Koutini & Widmer, 2023. Efficient large-scale audio tagging via transformer-to-CNN knowledge distillation. The compact taggers (mn04, mn10, mn20) in the charts.
  • Chen et al., 2022. BEATs: audio pre-training with acoustic tokenizers. One of the large self-taught listeners, and the one the diary system builds on.
  • Chen et al., 2024. EAT: self-supervised pre-training with efficient audio transformer. The model that hears every tap as nearly the same, and the one the Ear is fine-tuned from.
  • Dinkel et al., 2024. Scaling up masked audio encoder learning for general audio classification. Dasheng, the best of the bunch at hearing emotion in a voice.
  • Goel et al., 2025. Audio Flamingo 3: advancing audio intelligence with fully open large audio language models. The big model we asked about the week.
  • Dekkers et al., 2017. The SINS database for detection of daily activities in a home environment using an acoustic sensor network. The week in one home.
  • Radford et al., 2022. Robust speech recognition via large-scale weak supervision. Whisper.
  • Fitzgerald, 2010. Harmonic/percussive separation using median filtering. The smooth-and-sharp trick.
  • Rabiner, 1989. A tutorial on hidden Markov models and selected applications in speech recognition. Still the friendliest introduction to the Viterbi smoothing behind the diary.
  • Huh et al., 2023. EPIC-SOUNDS: a large-scale dataset of actions that sound. The kitchen recordings behind the short-events results.
  • Cao et al., 2014. CREMA-D: crowd-sourced emotional multimodal actors dataset. The acted voices behind the emotion scores in the appendix.

On writing things down instead of remembering:

  • Zeng et al., 2022. Socratic Models: composing zero-shot multimodal reasoning with language. Turns first-person video into a text log and answers questions by reading it. The earliest clear version of the diary idea I know of.
  • 2024. User-in-the-loop evaluation of multimodal LLMs for activity assistance. Finds that the text-log approach beats models looking at frames directly when a task needs a long history, including in a live cooking study.
  • 2024. VideoAgent: a memory-augmented multimodal agent for video understanding (ECCV). A structured memory of events and objects that a language model queries with tools.
  • 2026. Event-grounded question answering over long audio via structured retrieval. The audio version: long recordings become timestamped events in a database, with smart-home monitoring as a target use. The closest thing to this post's diary.
  • 2026. TAG-Bench: benchmarking temporal audio grounding in large audio language models. Twenty-one systems asked when sounds happen, with the best model counting repeated sounds correctly under 14% of the time. Independent evidence for the counting problem.
  • Sutton, 2019. The bitter lesson. Why a diary format designed by a person is probably a stepping stone, not the destination.

Recordings and licences

All recordings are from Freesound, transcoded to 16 kHz mono. The glass clink is CC BY 4.0 (KVV_Audio); everything else is CC0. The kettle's four states were picked out automatically from the recording's loudness envelope and checked against its description. The bouncing ball is synthesised.

Method

Three seeds, or a confidence interval reported with the number. Sources in the test set are held out from training. Thresholds, calibration and the test pass go through one code path, and sealed test sets are opened once, inside the runner, not while exploring. The SINS memory result is one home; the cost numbers are one core in fp32, not a phone. Audio Flamingo 3 was run on an A100 on the same 298 items.
