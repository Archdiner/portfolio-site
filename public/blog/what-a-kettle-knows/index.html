What a Kettle Knows

I tried to teach a computer to listen to a kitchen. Hearing turned out to be the easy part. The hard part was remembering.

Before you read anything else, put on headphones if you have them and press play. Don't look for clues. Just listen, and make a guess.

How did you know?

Someone flicks the kettle on about three seconds in. It rumbles, builds to a roar, boils for a minute and a half, and clicks itself off at 1:39. Most people get it, or get close, within a few seconds.

That's a little strange when you think about it. Nobody said the word "kettle". There was no picture. All that reached you was air pushing on your eardrums for a couple of minutes.

And you picked up more than a name. Without trying, you probably noticed that it started with a click, that the noise grew for a while before it settled into a steady roar, and that it stopped by itself with another click. If I asked how long it boiled, you could give me a rough answer. If I asked whether anyone poured the water afterwards, you'd say no.

Play it again and watch those moments go by.

That second kind of knowing is what this post is about. Putting a name on a sound is one skill. Keeping track of what happened, in what order and for how long, is another. People do both so naturally that we don't notice they're separate. Machines, it turns out, are far better at the first than the second.

Why would a computer need to hear a kettle?

Think about the questions people actually ask about a home. Did I leave the stove on? Has Dad made his morning tea yet, or is he still in bed? How long has the washing machine been beeping? If you're deaf, was that the kettle finishing or the smoke alarm?

None of these is "what is this sound?" They're about time. Is it on? Since when? For how long? How many times today? A small device that listens could answer them privately, without a camera in the kitchen. But only if it can turn a stream of sound into something like a diary.

So let's try to build that, starting from what a microphone actually hands us.

1.76 million numbers

A microphone measures how hard the air is pushing on it, over and over. This recording took that measurement 16,000 times every second. Each measurement is a single number, and the whole clip is a list of about 1.76 million of them.

Drag the slider to zoom in.

From far away you can see the shape of the event. Zoom into the boil and it turns to fuzz. Zoom further and you reach the individual numbers, each one a dot swinging up and down.

Nothing in this list says "kettle". There isn't even a number that means "loud". Loudness is how far the dots swing, measured across hundreds of them at once. This is all a computer gets, and everything that follows is about finding which patterns in these numbers matter and throwing the rest away.

Throwing most of it away

A good first question is how much we can throw away before the kettle stops sounding like a kettle. Here are the same twenty seconds three ways. The first is the original. The second keeps half as many numbers per second, roughly what a phone call carries. The third also strips out every pitch above 4,000 Hz, about the top note of a piano.

It gets duller, and the hiss of the boil is the first thing to go, but it's still obviously a kettle with a fraction of the data. A lot of those numbers were detail you never needed.

The picture under the player is the best way to see what changed. It's called a spectrogram, and it will come up again, so it's worth a proper look.

A picture of sound

Any sound can be treated as a mix of pitches playing at once: low rumbles, middle tones, high hiss. A spectrogram splits the sound into those pitches and shows how strong each one is over time. Time runs left to right, as in the waveform. Pitch runs from low at the bottom to high at the top. Brighter means louder.

Here is the kettle again, drawn both ways.

The switch is a thin vertical line. A click is so short that it contains a bit of every pitch at once, so it lights up a whole column. The boil is a broad smear, because thousands of bursting bubbles make noise at every pitch, all the time. Over the boil the top of the picture slowly dims: the roar gets duller as it goes on. Even after the click-off it doesn't go dark straight away, because the water keeps settling for a few seconds, and the colour drains out gradually.

When we cut the quality in the last section, we were shaving the top off this picture. The kettle survived because most of what makes it a kettle lives in the bottom half.1

Smooth and sharp

Once sound is a picture, simple geometry gets you surprisingly far. Here's a different recording: hot water going into a cup, with a spoon knocking against the side.

Steady sounds, like running water, draw horizontal stripes, because the same pitches carry on over time. Sharp sounds, like a spoon hitting china, draw vertical lines, because every pitch flares up for an instant. A classic trick separates a spectrogram into its horizontal and vertical parts.2 There's no learning and no training data involved, just "keep what's smooth side to side" and "keep what's smooth top to bottom".

It isn't perfect, and some water leaks into the clicks, but the spoon pops right out. Hold on to this. The vertical lines are what you'd count if someone asked how many times the spoon hit the cup, and counting is going to come back.

Which sounds are alike?

Pictures help, but a computer still can't tell that two sounds are the same just by comparing pixels. The same mug tapped softly and tapped hard makes two quite different pictures. What we want is a way to say "these are basically the same sound".

The usual answer is to train a model that turns any clip into a short list of numbers, called an embedding, so that clips which sound alike end up with similar lists. Then "how alike are these two sounds?" becomes a simple calculation. Here's a small test. Play the four taps.

To my ear, A and B are clearly the same mug, C is a glass and D is metal. Here is how alike four different listeners think each pair is, on a scale where 1 means identical.

The recipe has never been trained on anything. It measures a few dozen things a sound engineer would care about, like brightness and how fast a sound dies away. The other three are large models that learned by listening to millions of clips.3

All four agree that the two mug taps are the closest pair, which is reassuring. But look at how they spread the rest. The recipe thinks a soft mug tap and a metal bowl are quite different (0.56). EAT gives every pair 0.96 or more: to it, all four taps are nearly the same sound. On this tiny test, a recipe a person wrote by hand separates materials better than a model with 90 million learned settings.

Four clips don't prove much. What they show is that an embedding is a decision about what to ignore. EAT has decided that the difference between china and metal mostly doesn't matter. For plenty of tasks that's the right call. In a kitchen, it might not be.

Asking a trained ear

Let's see what a real model makes of the kettle. A while ago I trained a small listener, which I'll call the Ear, to pick out fifteen everyday home sounds: running water, a light switch, dishes clinking, a vacuum cleaner, and so on. Here's what it heard.

A light switch. Running water. A coffee machine. And then, for most of the boil, a vacuum cleaner.

It's easy to laugh, but look at the timing. "Light switch" lands at 2.7 seconds, exactly when the kettle clicks on. "Running water" and "coffee machine" cover the heating, when a kettle really does sound like water being moved around. "Kettle" isn't one of the Ear's fifteen words, so it reached for the nearest ones it had. It heard the sound fine. It just didn't have the vocabulary.

The more interesting thing is what it didn't say. After about 53 seconds it goes quiet, even though the kettle boils for another 45. A steady roar that never changes stops looking like news. And nothing in its output connects the click at the start to the silence at the end, or says that the same object was on the whole time.

That's the gap. A model like this answers "what is this sound?", a few seconds at a time. The questions from the start of the post, like how long it boiled or whether it's still on, need something that keeps track between those few seconds.

Noticing versus counting

Before building that, I wanted to know which basic skills the models already had. I collected 345 short clips where something changes partway through: a sound starts, stops, or gives way to another. Then I asked two things. Did the model notice the change? And could it count how many times something repeated?

Noticing goes fine. The best large model scores 0.78. And the hand-written recipe gets 0.74, ahead of every trained model but one.

Counting is a wipeout. No model scores above 0.14.

The reason is the embedding itself. It squashes a stretch of audio into one list of numbers, which is a bit like describing a song in one sentence. You can say it's upbeat and has a guitar in it, but not how many times the chorus came round. Try counting the bounces of this ball yourself.

You can do that easily, and so can a very old trick: slide the waveform along a copy of itself and see where it lines up.4 That trick scores 0.94 on the same counting test, where the best model managed 0.14. So the fix isn't a bigger model. It's using the right tool for each job: a model to say what, and plain signal tricks for when and how many.

Keep a diary

Here's the idea the rest of this post rests on. Instead of asking a model to describe audio, have the listener keep a diary. Every time something changes, write down what turned on or off, and when. Smooth over the flicker, so one long boil is one entry rather than thirty.5

Once the diary exists, the hard questions stop being hard. They're just arithmetic on a list. Here's the kettle's diary. Ask it something.

That's the kettle, with the diary written by hand. The real question is whether it works when a listener writes the diary itself, over days, in a real house.

To test it, I used SINS, a public dataset recorded continuously in one person's home for a week.6 The system listened to everything, kept its diary, and then answered 298 questions about days four to seven. When did the washing-up start? How long was the vacuum on? How long since anyone last cooked?

For comparison I asked Audio Flamingo 3 the same questions. It's a large audio-language model: you give it a recording and ask it things in plain English, which on paper is exactly what we want.

The diary gets 0.87 overall. Audio Flamingo 3 gets 0.19, which is worse than the 0.26 you'd get by giving the same answer every time without listening at all.

My best guess at why: Audio Flamingo is being asked to hold days of audio in its head at once, and nothing in it is built to do that. It scores zero on "how long ago" questions. The diary doesn't need to hold anything in its head, because it wrote things down as they happened.

The diary's weak spot is "how many" (0.41). That's the counting problem again, and it's the clearest next thing to fix. And this is one home. A second house, with different appliances and different habits, would be the real test.

Can you leave it on?

A diary is only useful if whatever writes it never stops listening. So how much does that cost? Here's a full day of listening, and how much of one ordinary computer processor each option needs to keep up.

A compact model of the size the Ear uses gets through a whole day of audio in about eight minutes of processor time. The full diary system, with its smoothing and bookkeeping, takes about an hour and forty minutes: roughly 7% of one core, running in the background. It does want up to 3.6 GB of memory at its peak, which is the next thing to bring down. But it's already in "leave it running on a small box in the corner" territory.

Voices, briefly

The same lesson turned up when the sound was speech. Whisper, a popular speech-to-text model, has a known habit: give it stretches with nobody talking and it makes words up. In my tests it invented 846 words for every hour of non-speech audio.

Putting a tiny speech detector in front of it, so that Whisper only hears the parts where someone is talking, took that to zero. On noisy recordings the error rate fell from 25% of words to 4%. The big model didn't change at all. A small listener deciding when to pay attention did all the work.7

What didn't work

Plenty didn't, and it's worth being specific:

  • Counting. Every learned listener failed the repetition test. For now the diary leans on the waveform trick, and it's still weakest on "how many" questions.
  • Flagging anything unusual. A detector for "a sound I've never heard before" raised about 41 false alarms a day and caught only half of the real novelties. Nobody would leave that switched on.
  • Listening again when unsure. Letting the system take a second, closer listen to the clips it wasn't confident about didn't help.
  • Practice drills. Training on synthetic listening exercises improved scores on the exercises and made the real tests flat or worse.
  • Short kitchen sounds. With recordings from only five kitchens, fancier models for brief events like a drawer closing did worse than the simplest baseline. There just wasn't enough data.

The full list, with numbers, is in the appendix.

Back to the kettle

Let's play it one last time. This time, watch the diary that a good listener should write.

So what does a kettle know? Not that it's a kettle. The Ear never worked that out, and it didn't need to. What matters is that something switched on at 0:02.7, boiled for 86 seconds, and switched itself off. They're small facts, but they're exactly the ones people ask about.

The models we have are already good at hearing. What they're missing is somewhere to write things down.

Method
